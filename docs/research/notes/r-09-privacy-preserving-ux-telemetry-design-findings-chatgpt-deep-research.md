## Executive recommendation

For `mena`, **there is a telemetry model worth having, but it is not conventional remote product analytics**. The right model is: no background network submission; no third-party analytics SDKs; no persistent user, device, or installation identity; a dedicated local store for coarse health and UX signals; and a manual “export diagnostic summary” flow for troubleshooting or explicitly volunteered product feedback. If the team cannot fund those guardrails, the right answer is to keep telemetry absent. citeturn6search24turn5search4turn0search2turn8search0

This recommendation matches the evaluation order you set. It maximises privacy preservation first, sharply reduces accidental content leakage second, still provides useful evidence for reliability and UX improvement third, preserves clear user control fourth, keeps the product fully local-first fifth, and keeps telemetry structurally separate from meeting artefacts sixth. It also avoids the common trap of treating “privacy-preserving telemetry” as synonymous with “remote analytics plus better wording”; for a meeting-notes product, that would be the wrong architectural default. citeturn6search24turn3search4turn4search0turn5search4

## Why automatic telemetry should stay absent

A local-first product derives much of its trust from the fact that **the network is optional and user data remains under the user’s control**. The local-first literature does not present privacy as a layer added after the fact; it treats privacy, offline operation, and user ownership as part of the core architectural bargain. Adding background telemetry to a product whose central promise is local sovereignty therefore imposes a much higher trust burden than it would in an ordinary cloud app. citeturn6search24

The regulatory and privacy-engineering context points in the same direction. GDPR treats online identifiers as potentially identifying, warns that device- and application-level identifiers can leave traces usable for profiling, and makes clear that pseudonymised data remains within scope when a person can still be singled out or linked. The EDPB’s anonymisation guidance uses **singling out, linkability, and inference** as the key tests, while NIST’s disassociability objective is to avoid associating data or events with individuals or devices beyond operational need. For `mena`, that means fields that may look “harmless” in isolation—precise timestamps, repeated installation IDs, exact meeting length, speaker-count signals, error chronology, model/backend combinations—can become identifying when combined. citeturn1search1turn1search5turn3search4turn3search5turn4search0turn5search4

Even comparatively permissive analytics guidance is much tighter than normal product teams assume. Guidance from entity["organization","CNIL","french privacy regulator"] allows some audience/performance measurement without consent only under narrow conditions: purpose limitation, no cross-checking with other processing, scope limited to a single publisher/app, no global tracking, user information and objection rights, and constrained retention. CNIL also explicitly distinguishes simple audience measurement from more intrusive practices such as heatmaps and session replay. A privacy-first meeting product should therefore adopt a boundary **stricter** than ordinary web/app analytics, not looser. citeturn0search1turn0search2turn0search3turn0search7

Advanced privacy-preserving remote measurement does exist, but it is the wrong move here. Reference work from entity["company","Apple","consumer tech company"] shows that useful aggregate learning can be done with opt-in differential privacy, no device identifiers, and no exact event timestamps in uploaded records; however, Apple’s own material also describes substantial utility/privacy trade-offs and, in newer deployments, uses secure aggregation with very large cohorts and two non-colluding server roles. As of 30 April 2026, the Distributed Aggregation Protocol effort at the entity["organization","IETF","internet standards body"] likewise assumes two aggregators and thresholded aggregate computation rather than simple app telemetry uploads. That is real engineering, but it is disproportionate for a Linux-first, local-first meeting-notes product and especially unjustified for a PoC. citeturn13search0turn9search1turn9search4turn10search6

## The only telemetry model worth having

If `mena` introduces telemetry later, it should define telemetry narrowly as **local product-health observability**, not behavioural surveillance and not content analytics. In practice, that means a small, dedicated local store containing only closed-schema data needed to answer questions like “where are users getting stuck?”, “which stage fails most often?”, and “what performance class is acceptable on real machines?” rather than “what happened in this meeting?” or “who was involved?”. This mirrors the most privacy-conscious parts of telemetry governance in systems from entity["organization","Mozilla","browser organization"]: declared metrics, transparency, metric expiry, and the ability to avoid sending client IDs. citeturn8search0turn7search0turn7search7

The **allowed categories** should be limited to the following.

- **Operational health and reliability.** Application version, enabled local models/backends, startup success/failure, stage success/failure classes, storage pressure, model-missing conditions, and coarse crash/error class counts are worth keeping because they help improve robustness while staying close to the “technical performance / problem detection” purposes that privacy regulators treat as the least intrusive form of analytics. Keep values categorical or bucketed, never free-form. citeturn0search2turn5search4turn8search0

- **Performance buckets, not exact traces.** Stage durations for recording, transcription, diarisation, and summarisation can be useful, but only as coarse ranges such as “under one minute”, “one to five minutes”, and “over five minutes”, plus similarly coarse CPU/memory-pressure classes. This matches the principle of limiting resolution rather than collecting unnecessarily exact measurements. citeturn0search2turn8search0turn13search0

- **Feature-flow counters.** Counts for actions like “recording started”, “recording stopped”, “summary requested”, “export used”, “settings opened”, or “fallback backend selected” are acceptable if they are closed-vocabulary events with no user-provided strings and no meeting-derived payload. These are the most useful UX signals that do not need content or identity. citeturn8search0turn7search0

- **Explicit user judgement, if volunteered.** A thumbs-up/down on a summary, an explicit “this failed me because latency was too high” reason code, or a small closed list such as “speaker split wrong / summary too vague / export unclear” can be valuable, but it should be **explicitly user-triggered** and ideally included only in a manual export package. Apple’s differential-privacy analytics material is helpful here mainly as a posture reference: opt-in participation, local visibility, and avoidance of hidden identifiers and exact event timestamps. citeturn13search0turn9search0

- **Meeting-shaped signals only under stricter conditions.** If the team ever concludes that some meeting-derived diagnostic is genuinely necessary—such as media-length bucket or coarse diarisation cardinality bucket—it should remain **local-only by default**, be heavily bucketed, and be included in off-device sharing only after explicit preview. Under the EDPB’s singling-out and linkability tests, these fields are not intrinsically safe merely because they are numerical. citeturn4search0turn3search4turn3search5

The trade-off is straightforward: this model will produce less broad behavioural insight than cloud analytics, and manual exports will bias evidence toward support incidents and motivated testers. That is acceptable for `mena`. The purpose here is not growth analytics; it is product improvement without breaking the privacy contract.

## Identifier and export posture

**No persistent identifier is justified for this product’s telemetry.** GDPR explicitly treats online identifiers as potentially identifying, and pseudonymisation does not magically convert linked records into anonymous data. For `mena`, a stable installation ID, device ID, account-linked ID, or derived participant token would create exactly the kind of silent cross-session trace the product says it does not want. citeturn1search1turn1search5turn3search4turn3search5turn4search0

The practical identifier posture should therefore be:

- **Inside the local store:** internal row keys or sequence numbers are acceptable only as purely local implementation artefacts with no meaning outside that store. They should not survive reset, should not be exposed as “user IDs”, and should never be transmitted anywhere automatically. citeturn5search4turn8search10

- **In exported diagnostic packages:** if an identifier is needed at all, use a **one-time export ID** generated when the user creates the package, plus package-local sequence numbers. Do not reuse that identifier across exports, and do not derive it from machine properties, account details, or meeting metadata. Apple’s privacy documentation is instructive here: where it must associate server-visible data at all, it uses random identifiers and even rotates some of them frequently rather than tying them to accounts. citeturn9search0turn13search0

- **For time data:** do not include precise event timestamps in exported telemetry summaries. Apple’s local-DP analytics records explicitly avoid sending device identifiers and the timestamps at which events were generated. `mena` should follow the same philosophy by preferring coarse date ranges, relative durations, or user-selected time windows over exact chronology. citeturn13search0

The **user-control posture** should be equally strict. Telemetry, if it exists, should remain local-only by default; users should be able to inspect it in a human-readable form, clear it entirely, and export it only by explicit action. Export should include a preview, a description of every field class being shared, and a choice of time range or category scope. Privacy-conscious telemetry systems also make deletion path and transparency first-class: Glean defines a deletion-request mechanism, and Apple surfaces its analytics records locally so users can inspect what exists on the device. citeturn7search7turn8search0turn13search0

If `mena` ever considers **true remote aggregate telemetry**, that should be treated as a separate future programme, not an extension of the PoC. At that point the minimum acceptable bar would be: hard opt-in, no client identifier, no exact event timestamps, published schemas, cohort thresholds, and ideally multi-party aggregation or equivalent privacy-preserving measurement infrastructure. Apple’s newer deployments and the IETF DAP work show the level of machinery that becomes relevant once sensitive aggregates are collected off-device. citeturn9search1turn9search4turn10search6

## Red lines to document now

The architecture should document several **non-negotiable red lines** up front.

- **Never collect meeting substance.** No transcript text, summary text, action items, extracted entities, embeddings, audio snippets, screenshots, semantic labels, or content-derived keywords. The fact that such material could be pseudonymised is irrelevant; pseudonymised data can still be linkable or attributable. citeturn1search5turn3search5turn4search0

- **Never collect participant identity or participant-linked surrogates.** No names, emails, calendar attendees, speaker IDs that survive beyond a single local processing session, voiceprints, or any token that can track the same person across meetings. Under GDPR and EDPB reasoning, those would be personal or at minimum strongly linkable data. citeturn1search1turn3search4turn4search0

- **Never collect free-form strings for telemetry.** That includes user comments, raw exception messages, prompt text, copied shell commands, meeting titles, filenames, folder names, file paths, URLs, hostnames, and argument strings. OWASP explicitly recommends excluding file paths, internal network names, and personal data from logs, and Glean’s own URL metric documentation warns that arbitrary URLs can contain identifying data such as directory paths or credentials. citeturn11search0turn8search11

- **Never ship crash dumps or memory-bearing diagnostics by default.** Mozilla’s docs note that minidumps can contain complete program memory and are therefore much more likely to contain private information. If crash insight is needed, use symbolic error classes and aggressively sanitised top-frame summaries, not raw dumps or memory snapshots. citeturn12search4turn12search6turn12search0

- **Never use session replay, heatmaps, or full behavioural chronology.** CNIL treats these as materially more intrusive than simple performance/statistical measurement. For a product handling meeting recordings and speech-derived artefacts, they are plainly over the line. citeturn0search7

- **Never rely on hashed or pseudonymous stable IDs as a workaround.** The law and regulator guidance do not treat pseudonymisation as anonymity, and the architecture should not pretend otherwise. citeturn1search5turn3search5turn4search0

## Changes required before activation

If telemetry is ever promoted from deferred to active, several architectural changes become necessary before the first event is recorded.

First, `mena` needs a **dedicated telemetry subsystem and storage boundary**. Telemetry should live in its own store, with its own retention and deletion semantics, outside the canonical meeting-artifact namespace. OWASP distinguishes operational/process logging from other traces because different purposes require different controls, and Glean likewise persists telemetry in a dedicated local tree rather than mixing it into ordinary content structures. For `mena`, the equivalent architectural rule should be: telemetry is derivative diagnostics, never a canonical meeting artefact. citeturn11search0turn8search10

Second, the product needs a **schema registry and privacy review gate** for every metric. Glean’s strongest design lessons are not its network pipeline but its governance: declared measurements only, automated documentation, expiration dates, optional omission of client IDs, and type-aware restriction of what can be recorded. `mena` should adopt that discipline internally: closed vocabulary only, no free-form strings, explicit owner and purpose for every metric, expiry/review dates, and a refusal to compile or ship undeclared metrics. citeturn8search0turn7search0

Third, telemetry activation requires **user-legibility features**, not just storage. There should be a local viewer, a reset/delete control, and an export builder that previews exactly what will be shared. Apple’s visible analytics records and Glean’s transparency/deletion posture both point to the same rule: if users cannot see and clear it, you should be suspicious of collecting it. citeturn13search0turn7search7turn8search0

Fourth, the engineering process needs **guardrails against accidental leakage**. That means automated tests or policy checks that reject telemetry fields containing raw text, paths, URLs, stable IDs, exact timestamps, or content-bearing debug artefacts; it also means ensuring that loss of network connectivity never degrades local operation, because the product must remain fully functional without any telemetry egress path. OWASP explicitly warns that logging failures should not cause broader application failure, and local-first architecture depends on network-optional operation. citeturn11search0turn6search24

Finally, if the product ever moves beyond local export into **real remote telemetry**, that is effectively a new architecture decision. At that point, `mena` would need explicit opt-in, published schema docs, server-side deletion semantics, thresholded aggregates, and likely a privacy-preserving aggregation design rather than ordinary client-event uploads. The current Apple and IETF material shows that doing this credibly is possible, but only with considerably more machinery than a PoC or early local-first client should carry. citeturn9search1turn9search4turn10search6

The unblock decision, then, is this: **for the current PoC, keep telemetry absent**. **For later versions, allow only a bounded local-only observability model with explicit manual export.** Do **not** introduce automatic outbound telemetry, stable identifiers, or content-adjacent analytics unless `mena` is willing to redesign around a much heavier privacy-measurement architecture.
